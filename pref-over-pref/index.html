Some preferences are stronger than others, but numerical scores for that strength are hard to give consistently. We ask a simpler question: of these two preferences, which one is stronger? Each answer gives the stronger preference a margin to beat, set by the weaker one. DPO-PoP learns those margins without ever seeing a numerical score.

The idea

Compare preferences, not just responses

A standard preference says response A beats response B. It does not say by how much. Margin-based losses can use that strength, but they usually get it from numerical ratings, and annotators who agree on which response is better often disagree on the scores.

So we ask a second-order question. Given two preferences, \(A \succ B\) and \(C \succ D\), which one reflects the larger difference? If the label says \((A \succ B) \succ (C \succ D)\), then under the true reward \(r\),

\[ r(A) - r(B) \;>\; r(C) - r(D). \]

The weaker preference's margin becomes a lower bound for the stronger one. The two preferences can come from different prompts, and the label is a single binary choice. This follows the same principle as MACBETH in decision analysis and best-worst scaling in annotation: relative judgments are easier to make consistently than absolute scores.

Read the abstract

Margin-based optimization is fundamental to improving generalization and robustness in classification tasks. In the context of reward model learning from preferences within Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), existing methods typically rely on no margins, fixed margins, or margins that are simplistic functions of preference ratings. However, such formulations often fail to account for the varying strengths of different preferences, and methods that do adapt the margin require access to numerical preference scores. We propose leveraging preferences over preferences, that is, annotations indicating which of two preferences reflects a stronger distinction, to infer adaptive margins on a per-datapoint basis, eliciting margin information without requiring annotators to assign numerical scores. Such preference-over-preference annotations are general and can be incorporated into both standard RLHF reward modeling objectives and direct alignment losses. As a concrete instantiation, we introduce DPO-PoP, an extension to Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) that incorporates adaptive margins from preference-over-preference supervision, improving generative performance over margin-based baselines both when PoP labels are derived from dataset scores and when they are gathered from an LLM annotator, with additional discriminative gains in the former setting. Additionally, we show a tradeoff between discriminative and generative performance and propose two sampling strategies for gathering preference-over-preference labels to navigate it.

Method

DPO-PoP: a margin from the weaker preference

The PoP objective fits a reward model or, as here, plugs into DPO. A PoP dataset holds pairs of preferences, a stronger one \((x_s, y_s^+, y_s^-)\) and a weaker one \((x_w, y_w^+, y_w^-)\).

Reward modeling with PoP
\[\min_{\phi}\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{PoP}}}\Big[-\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x_s,y_s^+) - r_\phi(x_s,y_s^-) - \text{sg}\big[r_\phi(x_w,y_w^+) - r_\phi(x_w,y_w^-)\big]\big)\Big]\]

A margin-based Bradley–Terry loss in which the margin is the model's own reward gap on the weaker preference.

Implicit margin
\[m_\theta(x, y^+, y^-) = \beta\Big(\log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y^+\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+\mid x)} - \log\tfrac{\pi_\theta(y^-\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^-\mid x)}\Big)\]

As in DPO, the reward is written in terms of the policy, so there is no separate reward model and no RL stage.

DPO-PoP objective
\[\min_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\text{PoP}}}\Big[-\log\sigma\Big(m_\theta(x_s,y_s^+,y_s^-) - \text{sg}\big[\text{clip}_{[0,M_{\max}]}\big(m_{\hat\theta}(x_w,y_w^+,y_w^-)\big)\big]\Big)\Big]\]

The stronger preference has to beat the weaker one's margin. After building the PoP pairs, the scalar scores are discarded and never used in training.

Stop-gradient

The weaker margin is a fixed target. Without \(\text{sg}\), the loss could be lowered by shrinking or inverting the weaker preference. Each PoP example backpropagates only through the stronger preference.

Clipping to \([0, M_{\max}]\)

Unbounded margins make the loss swing and occasionally explode. Clipping keeps the target in a fixed range and stabilizes optimization.

Polyak target policy

The weaker margin is computed with \(\pi_{\hat\theta}\), a slowly updated average of \(\pi_\theta\), so the targets do not move too fast between steps.

Cost: one extra gradient-free forward pass over the weaker preference, under the target and reference policies. In the paper's setting that is 1.24× the wall-clock time of DPO run for the same number of optimizer steps.

# Sketch of the DPO-PoP loss, written from the equation above (not the released code).
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def margin(logp, ref_logp, beta):
    # logp, ref_logp: (B, 2) sequence log-probs of [preferred, dispreferred]
    return beta * ((logp[:, 0] - ref_logp[:, 0]) - (logp[:, 1] - ref_logp[:, 1]))

def dpo_pop_loss(strong_logp, strong_ref, weak_logp_target, weak_ref, beta, m_max):
    m_s = margin(strong_logp, strong_ref, beta)                 # gradients flow here
    with torch.no_grad():                                      # stop-gradient
        m_w = margin(weak_logp_target, weak_ref, beta).clamp(0.0, m_max)
    return -F.logsigmoid(m_s - m_w).mean()

Building the PoP dataset

Which preferences get compared shapes what the model learns

A dataset of \(N\) preferences allows about \(N^2/2\) PoP pairs, but \(kN\) is enough (\(k = 2\) by default). The paper compares two ways of choosing them.

Iterative sampling → DPO-PoP-Iter

Each preference is compared with \(k\) weaker ones, so every preference (except the weakest few) is the stronger side equally often. Weak preferences get as much training as strong ones, against even weaker, noisier partners.

Favors discrimination. Higher test accuracy, mostly from small-margin examples, at some cost in generation quality.

Random sampling → DPO-PoP-Random

Pairs are drawn at random and the annotator picks the stronger side. Strong preferences win more comparisons, so they get more gradient. It is also easier to collect: one label per random pair.

Favors generation. Best win rates and margin correlations, with robust RewardBench scores.

The discrimination–generation tradeoff

Test accuracy vs UltraRM win rate, labels from dataset scores

Why does the most accurate model generate worse?

DPO-PoP-Iter classifies about 8 points more test preferences correctly, but nearly all of that gain is on small-margin examples, where the labels are least reliable. On strongly preferred pairs it does worse than the baselines. DPO-PoP-Random keeps the strong preferences right and has the highest correlation with the dataset's own margins (Pearson 0.364, Spearman 0.369).

The paper connects this to a generalization bound for adaptive-margin classifiers that grows with \(\sqrt{\sum_i 1/m_i^2}\). Many tiny margins, which is what iterative sampling produces, loosen it. The bound's assumptions don't hold exactly for DPO-PoP, so the paper treats this as intuition rather than proof.

Little data, many comparisons

UltraRM win rate vs vanilla DPO with 7,500 preferences, as the PoP dataset grows to \(k \times 7{,}500\) pairs

With few preferences, re-pairing them helps a lot. Both PoP variants peak at 77% (\(k = 4\) to \(8\)), 17 points above DPO-margin-gt, then decline as the model starts fitting the inferred margins too closely. Test accuracy goes the other way and is best at \(k = 1\) or \(2\).

What the evidence does and doesn't show. The accuracy gain of DPO-PoP-Iter may come mostly from how iterative sampling over-represents weak preferences, not from the PoP labels: with completely random labels (\(\epsilon = 0.5\)) its in-domain accuracy does not drop. Every other metric, for both variants, does degrade without real labels.

With GPT-4.1 labels the gains are in generation only. Test accuracy and RewardBench are on par with the baselines, and DPO-margin-gt-scaled has the best accuracy. GPT-4.1's PoP labels agree with the ordering implied by UltraFeedback scores 75.4% of the time, with 7.9% ties discarded.

Correlations with “true” margins use UltraFeedback's LLM-assigned scores as a proxy, so they are only available in this setting.
